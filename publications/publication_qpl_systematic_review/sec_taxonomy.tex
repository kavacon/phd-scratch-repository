\section{Taxonomy of quantum programming systems}\label{sec:taxonomy}
Earlier classification organised quantum programming languages by imperative, functional and formal semantic approaches~\cite{Gay2006QPLSurvey}.
The taxonomy instead distinguishes programmer-facing abstraction through programming requirements, primary artefacts and allocation of implementation responsibility, following the programming, execution and hardware separation identified in prior work~\cite{DiMatteo2024AbstractionHierarchy}.
Five tiers span explicit quantum instructions to the quantum-implicit target motivated in Section~\ref{sec:background}.

\subsection{Language type definitions}\label{subsec:taxonomy:definitions}
Tier definitions concern what programmers must understand and author, not merely what a system can produce.
The comparison considers the visibility of quantum-mechanical concerns and the division of quantum realisation between programmer and implementation.
Descriptive system roles do not determine assessed tiers, which depend on representative programming evidence.

\begin{description}
    \item[Assembly]
    Assembly-tier systems provide explicit gates, measurements and classical control for interchange and execution~\cite{cross2022openqasm3}.
    Programmers author circuits over visible qubit registers, with hardware-control details available at the surface.
    Portability follows instruction semantics rather than language-level data abstraction.

    \item[Intermediate representation (IR)]
    IR-tier systems provide compiler and tooling authors with representations for interchange, lowering, optimisation and analysis~\cite{qir_alliance_qir_spec_repo,McCaskey2021MLIRDialectQASM}.
    Explicit resource models expose qubit lifetime and quantum/classical distinctions.
    Progressive lowering can preserve different levels of structure before and after gate decomposition, so audience and compilation role distinguish the tier rather than a fixed stack position~\cite{McCaskey2021MLIRDialectQASM}.

    \item[Framework]
    Framework-tier systems provide library or SDK interfaces for constructing circuit objects or quantum nodes through API calls~\cite{aleksandrowicz2019qiskit,bergholm2018pennylane}.
    A host language may supply control flow, data structures and tooling, while transpilation and backend interfaces support execution portability.
    Circuit-centred authoring defines the tier, regardless of whether its syntax is embedded or standalone.

    \item[High-level]
    High-level systems provide quantum-explicit programs structured through functions, modules and types rather than circuit files~\cite{bichsel2020silq,MicrosoftQSharpOverview,vax2025qmodexpressivehighlevelquantum}.
    Safety disciplines such as linearity, affinity or ownership govern resource use, with an explicit uncomputation or disposal account.
    Compiler assistance manages implementation obligations, while hardware-specific tuning remains optional.

    \item[Quantum-implicit]
    Quantum-implicit systems express programmer intent through ordinary mathematical and computational abstractions~\cite{nunez_corrales2025betterabstractmachines}.
    Representative programming does not require explicit qubits, gates or quantum-mechanical analogues.
    The compiler infers and synthesises quantum realisation, lowering it to an explicit IR or circuit.
    The implementation manages result extraction without requiring programmers to understand or explicitly cross a measurement or observation boundary.
    Optional expert access supports fine-grained control, optimisation and hardware-specific tuning.
\end{description}

\begin{mdframed}[frametitle={Litmus test]
A language is quantum-implicit if a programmer with no prior instruction in quantum mechanics can learn it from its own documentation.
That programmer must be able to implement representative programs without needing to understand qubits, gates, amplitudes or entanglement.
\end{mdframed}

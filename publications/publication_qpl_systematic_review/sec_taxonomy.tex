\section{Taxonomy of quantum programming systems}\label{sec:taxonomy}
Earlier classification organised quantum programming languages by imperative, functional and formal semantic approaches rather than by the programmer-facing abstraction requirements used here~\cite{Gay2006QPLSurvey}.
The taxonomy distinguishes quantum programming systems by their programming requirements, primary artefacts and allocation of implementation responsibility.
It operationalises the programming, execution and hardware separation identified in prior work~\cite{DiMatteo2024AbstractionHierarchy}.
Five tiers describe the spectrum from explicit quantum instructions to quantum-implicit programming.
The latter provides explicit criteria for the abstraction target motivated in Section~\ref{sec:background}.
Eleven systems are assessed against this taxonomy under the selection criteria defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

\subsection{Language type definitions}\label{subsec:taxonomy:definitions}
Tier definitions concern what programmers must understand and author, not merely what a system can produce.
Two dimensions organise the comparison: the visibility of quantum-mechanical concerns at the programming surface and the division of quantum realisation between programmer and implementation.
These dimensions follow the abstraction hierarchy and its account of bleed-through~\cite{DiMatteo2024AbstractionHierarchy}.
System roles provide descriptive context, while assessed tier assignments depend on the representative programming evidence.

\begin{description}
    \item[Assembly]
    Assembly-tier systems provide explicit quantum instructions comprising gates, measurements and classical control for interchange and execution~\cite{cross2022openqasm3}.
    The primary artefact is a circuit with classical control.
    Programmers specify quantum operations over visible qubit registers, with hardware-control details available at the surface.
    Portability follows instruction semantics rather than language-level data abstraction.
    Assembly supplies the instruction-level reference point for the spectrum.

    \item[Intermediate representation (IR)]
    IR-tier systems target compiler and tooling authors.
    They provide interchange, lowering, optimisation and analysis representations with explicit quantum resource models~\cite{qir_alliance_qir_spec_repo,McCaskey2021MLIRDialectQASM}.
    Their formal structure supports validation and exposes qubit lifetime and quantum/classical distinctions.
    Quantum IRs can preserve different levels of structure before and after gate decomposition through progressive lowering~\cite{McCaskey2021MLIRDialectQASM}.
    Audience and compilation role therefore distinguish the tier more precisely than a single fixed stack position.

    \item[Framework]
    Framework-tier systems provide library or SDK interfaces in which programmers construct circuit objects or quantum nodes through API calls~\cite{aleksandrowicz2019qiskit,bergholm2018pennylane}.
    A general-purpose host language may supply control flow, data structures and tooling.
    Quantum abstraction is API-driven, with transpilation and backend interfaces supporting execution portability.
    The defining boundary is circuit-centred authoring, rather than whether the system uses an embedded or standalone syntax.
    High-level abstraction instead treats circuit construction as an implementation detail.

    \item[High-level]
    High-level systems remain quantum-explicit but provide algorithmic structure through functions, modules and types~\cite{bichsel2020silq,MicrosoftQSharpOverview,vax2025qmodexpressivehighlevelquantum}.
    The primary artefact is a quantum program rather than a circuit file.
    Safety disciplines such as linearity, affinity or ownership govern resource use, together with an explicit uncomputation or disposal account.
    Compiler assistance manages implementation obligations, while hardware-specific tuning remains optional.
    Programmers nevertheless require quantum vocabulary to write correct programs.

    \item[Quantum-implicit]
    Quantum-implicit systems express their primary surface model through ordinary mathematical and computational abstractions~\cite{nunez_corrales2025betterabstractmachines}.
    Representative programming does not require explicit qubits, gates or quantum-mechanical analogues.
    The compiler infers and synthesises quantum realisation, lowering it to an explicit IR or circuit.
    Result extraction is managed by the implementation without requiring programmers to understand or explicitly cross a measurement or observation boundary.
    Quantum vocabulary is therefore non-mandatory in representative use.
    Explicit expert access remains available for fine-grained control, optimisation and hardware-specific tuning.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{The quantum-implicit tier}\label{subsubsec:taxonomy:quantum_implicit}
Quantum-implicitness distinguishes mathematical and computational authoring from structured programming that still requires quantum concepts.
Functions, transformations and distributions describe programmer intent without making circuit construction the default representation.
For example, an unstructured search can be expressed through a predicate over a domain, with the compiler synthesising the oracle and amplitude-amplification schedule~\cite{nunez_corrales2025betterabstractmachines}.
The programmer need not specify Hadamard gates or phase operations.

\begin{mdframed}
            [frametitle={Litmus test]
    A language is quantum-implicit if a programmer with no prior instruction in quantum mechanics can learn it from its own documentation.
    That programmer must be able to implement representative programs without needing to understand qubits, gates, amplitudes or entanglement.
\end{mdframed}

The tier turns the literature's abstraction requirements into a checkable classification.
Its entry criteria concern the joint absence of mandatory quantum obligations, not isolated conveniences or individual task successes.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_instrument} operationalises those criteria through representative tasks, condition profiles and explicit gating constraints.
